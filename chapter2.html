Chapter 2 — Probability

The language of randomness: sample spaces, events, counting outcomes, and updating probabilities with Bayes' theorem.

2.1 Sample space and events

  • Random experiment: a process whose outcome cannot be known before it is performed.
  • Sample space $S$: set of all possible outcomes.
  • Event: a subset of $S$. The event $A$ occurs if the outcome belongs to $A$.

Set operations on events

OperationMeaningNotation
Union$A$ or $B$ (or both)$A \cup B$
Intersectionboth $A$ and $B$$A \cap B$ (or $AB$)
Complement$A$ does not occur$A^c$
Difference$A$ but not $B$$A - B = A \cap B^c$
  • Events whose union is $S$ are exhaustive.
  • Events with no common element are disjoint (mutually exclusive).
  • $A$ and $A^c$ are both disjoint and exhaustive.
De Morgan's laws $$(A \cap B)^c = A^c \cup B^c, \qquad (A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c$$ In words: "not (A and B)" = "not A or not B". Very useful with the complement rule: $P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - P(\text{none})$.

2.2 Probability — Kolmogorov axioms

The three axioms A probability $P$ on $S$ assigns $P(E) \in \mathbb{R}$ to each event $E$ such that:
  1. Non-negativity: $P(E) \ge 0$.
  2. Normalization: $P(S) = 1$.
  3. Countable additivity: for pairwise disjoint $E_1, E_2, \ldots$, $$P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} E_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(E_i).$$

Properties that follow

  • $P(\emptyset) = 0$
  • $P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$ — the complement rule
  • Monotonicity: $A \subseteq B \Rightarrow P(A) \le P(B)$
  • $0 \le P(A) \le 1$ (not an axiom — it follows from the axioms)

Addition rule and inclusion–exclusion

Two events $$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$ Subtract $P(A \cap B)$ because adding $P(A) + P(B)$ counts the overlap twice. If $A$ and $B$ are disjoint, $P(A \cap B) = 0$ and the formula reduces to $P(A) + P(B)$.
Three events (inclusion–exclusion) $$P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup A_3) = \sum P(A_i) - \sum_{i
Worked example — cards From a 52-card deck:
  • Jack or three (disjoint): $P = \tfrac{4}{52} + \tfrac{4}{52} = \tfrac{8}{52}$
  • Jack or red card (overlap: 2 red jacks): $P = \tfrac{4}{52} + \tfrac{26}{52} - \tfrac{2}{52} = \tfrac{28}{52}$
Worked example — two job offers $P(A) = 0.8$, $P(B) = 0.6$, $P(A \cap B) = 0.5$.
  • At least one offer: $0.8 + 0.6 - 0.5 = 0.9$
  • Exactly one: $0.9 - 0.5 = 0.4$
  • No offer: $1 - 0.9 = 0.1$

2.3 Counting techniques

Equally likely outcomes If $S$ has $N$ equally likely outcomes, then for any event $E$: $$P(E) = \frac{|E|}{N} = \frac{\text{number of outcomes in } E}{N}$$
Pitfall This formula requires the outcomes to be equally likely. Toss a coin twice and record the number of heads: $S = \{0, 1, 2\}$, but $P(1) = 1/2 \ne 1/3$. The outcomes are not equally likely.

Counting principles

  • Addition principle: if a task can be done in one of several non-overlapping ways with $m_1, m_2, \ldots$ outcomes, the total is $m_1 + m_2 + \cdots$.
  • Multiplication principle: if a task has $n$ sequential steps with $m_1, \ldots, m_n$ outcomes, the total is $m_1 \cdot m_2 \cdots m_n$.

Decision rule: ask "does order matter?" President/treasurer → permutation. Choosing a 5-person committee → combination.

Worked example — club officers (n = 50) Choose president and treasurer:
  • No restrictions: $50 \times 49 = 2450$.
  • Person A serves only if president: (A pres: 49) + (A not chosen: $49 \times 48$) = 49 + 2352 = 2401.
  • B and C together or not at all: (both: 2) + (neither: $48 \times 47$) = 2 + 2256 = 2258.
  • D and E not together: $2450 - 2 = 2448$.
Worked example — arrangements of STATISTICS 10 letters with S×3, T×3, I×2, A×1, C×1: $$\frac{10!}{3!\,3!\,2!} = 50{,}400$$
Worked example — hotel rooms 7 students into 1 triple and 2 doubles: $$\binom{7}{3,2,2} = \frac{7!}{3!\,2!\,2!} = 210$$

The birthday problem — the "at least" trick

In a room of $n$ people, what is the probability that at least two share a birthday? Use the complement:

$$P(\text{at least two share}) = 1 - \frac{365 \times 364 \times \cdots \times (365 - n + 1)}{365^n}$$
$n$2023253050100
$P$0.410.510.570.710.970.9999997

General rule for "at least" problems

Compute the complement: $P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - P(\text{none})$. It is almost always easier than enumerating all the "at least" cases.

2.4 Conditional probability

Definition For events $A, B$ with $P(B) > 0$: $$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$ Intuition: $B$ becomes the new sample space, rescaled so $P(B \mid B) = 1$.
Multiplication (product) rule $$P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) \, P(B) = P(B \mid A) \, P(A)$$ For $n$ events (chain rule): $$P(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_n) = P(A_1) \cdot P(A_2 \mid A_1) \cdot P(A_3 \mid A_1 A_2) \cdots P(A_n \mid A_1 \cdots A_{n-1})$$
Worked example — fuses A box has 20 fuses, 5 defective. Three removed without replacement — all defective? $$P = \frac{5}{20} \cdot \frac{4}{19} \cdot \frac{3}{18} = \frac{1}{114}$$

Properties of conditional probability

Conditional probability behaves like an ordinary probability on the conditioning event:

  • $P(A \mid B) = 1 - P(A^c \mid B)$
  • $P(A \cup B \mid D) = P(A \mid D) + P(B \mid D) - P(A \cap B \mid D)$
  • $P(A \cap B \mid D) = P(A \mid B \cap D) \cdot P(B \mid D)$

Law of total probability

Events $B_1, \ldots, B_n$ form a partition of $S$ if they are pairwise disjoint and $\bigcup B_i = S$. ($B$ and $B^c$ are the simplest partition.)

Law of total probability If $B_1, \ldots, B_n$ partition $S$ with $0 < P(B_i) < 1$, then for any event $A$: $$P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_i) \, P(B_i)$$

Tree-diagram method: draw first-level branches for each $B_i$ labeled $P(B_i)$, and second-level branches for $A$ labeled $P(A \mid B_i)$. Multiply along each path ending in $A$, then add the paths.

Worked example — defective products Three machines I, II, III make 30%, 45%, 25% of products with defect rates 2%, 3%, 2%. $$P(\text{defective}) = 0.30(0.02) + 0.45(0.03) + 0.25(0.02) = 0.0245$$

Bayes' theorem

Bayes — short form $$P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B)\, P(B)}{P(A)}$$
Bayes — full form (with partition) If $B_1, \ldots, B_n$ partition $S$ with $0 < P(B_i) < 1$: $$P(B_j \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_j) \, P(B_j)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_i) \, P(B_i)}$$ Numerator is one tree path; denominator is the sum of all paths leading to $A$.

Prior vs. posterior

  • $P(B_j)$ is the prior — before you know $A$ happened.
  • $P(B_j \mid A)$ is the posterior — after you incorporate the evidence $A$.
Worked example — which machine made the defective product? Continuing the defective-products example, given a product is defective: $$P(\text{III} \mid \text{def}) = \frac{0.25 \times 0.02}{0.0245} = \frac{10}{49} \approx 0.204$$ Similarly $P(\text{I} \mid \text{def}) \approx 0.245$ and $P(\text{II} \mid \text{def}) \approx 0.551$. Machine II makes 45% of products but accounts for 55% of defectives.
Worked example — diagnostic test (base-rate fallacy) Prevalence $P(B) = 0.0004$. Sensitivity $P(A \mid B) = 0.95$. Specificity $P(A^c \mid B^c) = 0.90$, so $P(A \mid B^c) = 0.10$. $$P(B \mid A) = \frac{0.95 \times 0.0004}{0.95 \times 0.0004 + 0.10 \times 0.9996} \approx 0.0038$$ Even after a positive test, the probability of being infected is only 0.38%! The infection is so rare that almost all positives are false positives. The test still raises the probability ~10-fold (from 0.04% to 0.38%).
Worked example — Monty Hall Three doors, car behind one. You pick Door I. Host (who knows) opens Door III (goat). Switch to Door II?
  • $P(B_i) = 1/3$, $P(A \mid B_1) = 1/2$, $P(A \mid B_2) = 1$, $P(A \mid B_3) = 0$.
  • $P(A) = 1/2$
  • $P(B_1 \mid A) = \dfrac{(1/3)(1/2)}{1/2} = \dfrac{1}{3}$ (stick)
  • $P(B_2 \mid A) = \dfrac{(1/3)(1)}{1/2} = \dfrac{2}{3}$ (switch)
Always switch — it doubles your probability of winning.

2.5 Independence

Definition Events $A$ and $B$ are independent if $$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \, P(B)$$ Equivalently (when $P(B) > 0$): $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$ — knowing $B$ does not change the probability of $A$.
  • If $A, B$ are independent, so are $A$ and $B^c$, $A^c$ and $B$, and $A^c$ and $B^c$.
  • $A_1, \ldots, A_n$ are mutually independent if the product rule holds for every subcollection.
Pitfall — independent ≠ disjoint If $A, B$ are disjoint with $P(A), P(B) > 0$, then $P(A \cap B) = 0 \ne P(A)P(B)$, so they are dependent. If $A$ happened, you know $B$ didn't — that's the opposite of independence.

Chapter 2 pitfalls — the T/F traps

  1. Using $|E|/N$ when outcomes are not equally likely.
  2. Forgetting to subtract $P(A \cap B)$ in the addition rule when events overlap.
  3. Mixing up permutations (order matters) and combinations (order does not).
  4. Confusing $P(A \mid B)$ with $P(B \mid A)$ — the diagnostic-test example shows how different they can be.
  5. Confusing disjoint with independent — they are nearly opposites.
  6. Forgetting that the conditioning event changes the sample space (new denominator).
  7. Doing "at least one/two" problems directly — use the complement.
  8. Thinking $P(E) \le 1$ is an axiom — it follows from them.
  9. Writing "complementary ⇒ disjoint" (true) and "disjoint ⇒ complementary" (false — need union to be $S$ too).
